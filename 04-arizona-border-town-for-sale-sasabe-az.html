Arizona Border Town for Sale: Sasabe, Arizona

By Nathan Morrison, Century 21 Indie Realty

Part of Sasabe, Arizona, is on the market as one offering: about 441.59 acres across three parcels, listed at $3,500,000.

That is the listing this article is about. It is not a single house, and it is not a vacant lot. It is the town center of Sasabe — the historic general store, the bar, and the post office — plus tenant-occupied homes and hundreds of acres of open land around them, sold together as one offering.

What “A Border Town for Sale” Means Here

To be plain about it: this is part of Sasabe, not the whole town. Sasabe is a small, living community, and people live and work there. What is for sale is the town-center property described in the listing — three parcels totaling about 441.59 acres, listed July 29, 2026 — sold as one offering rather than piece by piece.

What Is Included

What you get is the working heart of the town. The store sits in a building from around 1920. Next to it are the Hill Top Bar and the post office — Sasabe’s own post office, ZIP code 85633. There are also homes on the property with people living in them as tenants, and open land around the town center.

The store, the bar, and the post office are still part of daily life in Sasabe. That is what makes this different from a plain land listing: a buyer is buying a working town center, not just acreage.

Where Sasabe Is

You will find Sasabe at the south end of Pima County, right on the border with Mexico. There is no mayor and no town council here — Sasabe is not its own town in that sense. Pima County is the local government.

The way in is State Route 286. It runs about 45 miles north to State Route 86 near Three Points. Out here the neighbors are ranches and the Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge. The closest town of any size is a long drive, so come and see the distance for yourself before you decide.

What a Buyer Should Check First

A town center is not a house, and it is not empty land either. Before you make an offer, check the things that decide whether you can run it the way you want to.

Access and easements. Find out who else can come onto the land, and how. There are easements tied to border construction, and federal access issues on the property. Learn what each one covers and what it allows, before you plan anything around them.

Boundaries, zoning, and permitted uses. Check what land and buildings are actually in the deal. Look at the three parcel lines, the zoning, and what each piece can be used for. Ranch land in unincorporated Pima County does not follow the same rules as a lot in town, and the store, bar, and post office each sit on the property in their own way — confirm which parcel holds what.

Utilities and buildings. The store, the bar, the post office, and the tenant homes are old, and they do not all run on the same services. Walk each building, ask what serves it, and do not take any of it from the listing alone.

That is not a reason to walk away. It is a reason to walk the property with the listing agent first.

Who This Fits

This is not the kind of purchase you start with. It suits someone who wants a big piece of historic border country and knows what that means — a small town that is still lived in, old buildings that need looking after, and a long drive to the next town.

It also suits someone who wants the town center to stay a town center — store, bar, and post office open and in use. Buyers have already looked at it that way, as one whole purchase, including a two-person partnership.

It does not fit someone looking for a subdivision lot, a quick flip, or a property with utilities at the street. This is a large, rural, border-country property, and a buyer needs to look at it that way.

How to See It

The only real way to judge it is to go. Drive in on State Route 286, walk through the store and the bar, stand in front of the post office, and look at the land past the town center. A photo and an acreage number will not tell you what it feels like to be that far out.
